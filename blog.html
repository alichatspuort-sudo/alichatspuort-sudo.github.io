How to Write Prompts That Actually Work

Most people get mediocre results from AI tools for the same reason: the prompt is too vague. "Write me a marketing email" gives the model almost nothing to work with, so it fills in the gaps with generic assumptions. A few small changes to how you phrase a request can make the difference between a throwaway answer and something you can actually use.

Here is a simple structure that works across almost every AI chat tool:

  • Give it a role. "Act as a senior email copywriter" nudges the model toward a more specific voice than a blank request.
  • State the goal, not just the topic. "Write an email that gets people to book a free trial" is more useful than "write an email about our product."
  • Add constraints. Word count, tone, audience, and format (bullet points vs paragraphs) all narrow the output toward what you actually need.
  • Give it an example when you can. Pasting one sentence in the voice you want is often worth more than three sentences of description.
  • Ask it to critique its own first draft. Following up with "what's the weakest part of this?" often produces a sharper second version than starting over.

None of this requires special access or a paid tool — it works the same way in ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini. The Best Prompts page on this site has 100 ready-made examples built around this same structure, organized by category, if you'd rather start from a template than write one from scratch.

AI Image Generators Compared: Which One Should You Use?

There are dozens of AI image tools now, but most people only need to know three or four of them well. Here's a plain-language breakdown of where each one tends to shine, based on what they're generally used for.

Midjourney is the go-to for stylized, artistic images — concept art, moody portraits, fantasy scenes. It has a distinct aesthetic quality that's hard to replicate elsewhere, though it runs through Discord rather than a standalone website, which trips up some first-time users.

DALL·E, built into ChatGPT, is the easiest entry point if you already use ChatGPT for other tasks — you can describe an image mid-conversation without switching tools, and it's reasonably good at following detailed instructions.

Adobe Firefly is the practical choice for anyone already working in Photoshop, since it's built directly into the app for tasks like extending backgrounds or removing objects, not just generating images from scratch.

Ideogram stands out for one specific thing: it's noticeably better than most competitors at rendering readable text inside an image, which matters for posters, logos, or memes.

If you're not sure where to start, DALL·E inside ChatGPT is the lowest-friction option. Once you know what look you're after, Midjourney is usually the upgrade people reach for next. 5 Ways to Use AI Chatbots Beyond Just Chatting

Most people use ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini the same way they'd use a search engine — ask a question, get an answer, move on. That's fine, but it undersells what these tools can actually do. A few less obvious uses:

  • Second opinion on a decision. Paste in the situation and ask it to argue the other side. It's a fast way to stress-test a plan before you commit to it.
  • Turning messy notes into something usable. Voice-to-text notes, meeting scribbles, or half-finished bullet points can be turned into a clean summary or action list in seconds.
  • Practicing a hard conversation. Ask the model to play the other person — a difficult client, a landlord, an interviewer — and rehearse what you'll say.
  • Explaining your own work back to you. Paste in a document you wrote and ask "what would a confused reader misunderstand here?" It catches gaps you can't see because you already know what you meant.
  • Long-document Q&A. Instead of reading a 40-page PDF, paste it in and ask targeted questions — most modern chat tools can hold surprisingly long documents in a single conversation.

None of these need a paid plan or special setup — just a different question than the one you'd usually ask.

Free vs Paid AI Tools: When Is It Worth Upgrading?

Nearly every AI tool now has a free tier, and for most casual use, that's genuinely enough. The upgrade is usually worth it once you hit one of these three limits, rather than on a fixed schedule.

You keep hitting a usage cap. If you're regularly told to wait, or bumped to a weaker model after a few messages, the free tier isn't built for your volume of use — that's the clearest signal to upgrade.

You need the newest model for accuracy-sensitive work. Free tiers often default to an older or smaller model. For coding, research, or anything where mistakes are costly, the latest model usually pays for itself quickly.

You need a feature that's paywalled outright. Longer memory, larger file uploads, priority access during peak times, or higher-resolution image generation are commonly locked behind a subscription rather than a usage limit.

If none of those apply — you use the tool occasionally, for simple tasks, and never see a wait message — a paid plan probably won't change much for you. It's worth revisiting the decision every few months rather than committing indefinitely either way, since free tiers change often.
